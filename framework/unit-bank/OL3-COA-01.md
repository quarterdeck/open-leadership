# OL3-COA-01: Coaching skills for managers

## Purpose

This unit enables the learner to use listening, questions and feedback to coach a person at work.

## Learning outcomes and assessment criteria

The learner must meet each criterion.

| Learning outcome | Assessment criteria |
|---|---|
| 1. Explain what coaching is and when a manager can use it | 1.1 Describe the difference between coaching, instruction, advice and mentoring<br>1.2 Identify situations at work in which coaching is suitable and situations in which it is not<br>1.3 Explain the limits that the role of line manager puts on a coaching conversation |
| 2. Use listening, questions and feedback in a coaching conversation | 2.1 Use open questions that help a person to think for themselves<br>2.2 Use summaries, silence and checks of understanding to show that they listen<br>2.3 Use feedback that describes what the person did and the effect that it had<br>2.4 Apply a simple structure to a conversation, from the aim to the action that the person chooses |
| 3. Review their own use of coaching skills | 3.1 Identify what worked and what did not work in a coaching conversation, with feedback from the person<br>3.2 Plan what to change in their next coaching conversation |

## Indicative content

This content is a guide for the centre. It is not a list of things to assess.

**Outcome 1.** Coaching as help for a person to find their own answer. Instruction, advice and
mentoring as different kinds of help. When to tell and when to ask: urgency, safety, the
experience of the person, the cost of an error. Short coaching moments in daily work compared
with a planned conversation. The manager also judges performance, so the person can hold things
back. What a manager can keep private and what they must report.

**Outcome 2.** Open, closed and leading questions. Questions that start with "what" and "how".
The habit of giving the answer, and how to stop it. Attention: no phone, no screen, eye contact,
time. Summaries in the words of the person. Silence as thinking time. Feedback that is specific,
prompt and about the work. The stages of a conversation in plain words: what the person wants,
what happens now, what they can do, what they will do and when. The person chooses the action.

**Outcome 3.** Notes made soon after a conversation. The share of talking time between the
manager and the person. Questions to ask the person about how useful the conversation was.
Common faults: advice dressed as a question, too many questions at once, no agreed action. One
change at a time.

## Workplace scenario

Use this scenario for discussion, or for assessment if the learner cannot use a case from their
own work.

Dylan leads the trade counter team of six people at a builders' merchant. He knows the stock
better than anybody and answers each question that the team brings him. The queue at his desk
gets longer each week. Sian joined four months ago. She is quick and careful, but she asks Dylan
before each quote for a large order, even when she has the correct price. Last week a customer
left because he waited too long. The branch manager wants Sian to do quotes alone by the end of
the month.

Tasks:

1. Explain why coaching is more suitable than instruction for Sian's quotes.
2. Plan a 15-minute coaching conversation with Sian. Write five open questions that Dylan can
   use.
3. Describe the feedback that Dylan gives Sian after her next large quote.

## Assessment

Suitable methods: observation at work, witness statement, reflective account, professional
discussion.

Evidence requirements:

- The evidence comes from at least three coaching conversations with one or more people at
  work, with a total of at least two hours.
- An assessor or a competent witness observes at least one conversation, with the consent of
  the person coached, or reviews a recording made with that consent.
- A reflective account covers each conversation and includes feedback from the person coached.
- The names of the people coached do not appear in the evidence.

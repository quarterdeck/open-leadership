## Purpose

This unit enables the learner to evaluate and apply approaches to pay, benefits and recognition.

## Learning outcomes and assessment criteria

The learner must meet each criterion.

| Learning outcome | Assessment criteria |
|---|---|
| 1. Analyse how reward supports the aims of an organisation | 1.1 Analyse the parts of reward in their organisation: pay, benefits, recognition and the rewards that are not financial<br>1.2 Analyse the internal and external factors that shape reward decisions<br>1.3 Evaluate how well the present reward practice supports the aims and values of the organisation |
| 2. Evaluate approaches to pay and benefits | 2.1 Evaluate methods to set the relative value of jobs in an organisation<br>2.2 Evaluate approaches to pay progression and to variable pay<br>2.3 Apply market data to assess the pay of a role or a group of roles<br>2.4 Assess the fairness of pay between groups of employees, with reference to UK equal pay and equality law |
| 3. Plan a change to reward or recognition practice | 3.1 Evaluate the evidence on how pay and recognition affect motivation and behaviour<br>3.2 Plan a change to a reward or recognition practice, with its costs, benefits and risks<br>3.3 Plan the communication of the change to managers and employees |
| 4. Evaluate their own practice in reward | 4.1 Evaluate the effect of a reward decision that they made or advised on<br>4.2 Plan changes to their practice, with reasons |

## Indicative content

This content is a guide for the centre. It is not a list of things to assess.

**Outcome 1.** Base pay, variable pay, benefits, pension, leave, flexibility, development,
recognition and the work itself. External factors: the labour market, minimum wage law, cost of
living, competitors, funding. Internal factors: what the organisation can afford, its values,
its pay history, agreements with unions. Whether to lead, match or follow the market. Signs that
reward works against the aims: a sales bonus that harms service, long service pay in a firm that
needs new skills.

**Outcome 2.** Relative value of jobs: job evaluation by factors and points, ranking, market
pricing, and the strengths and limits of each. Pay structures: spot rates, grades, broad bands,
job families. Progression by service, skill, performance or market movement. Bonuses,
commission and profit share, and their side effects. Sources of market data, the match of jobs
and the age of the data. Benefits: cost, value to employees, take-up, choice. Equal pay for
equal work, pay gap reporting, and the difference between the two. Pay audits. Transparency of
pay ranges.

**Outcome 3.** Evidence on money and motivation: pay that feels unfair demotivates, and more
pay does not always motivate. Rewards that crowd out the wish to do good work. Comparison with
colleagues matters as much as the amount. Recognition: prompt, specific, sincere, from a manager
or a colleague, public or private by preference. Cost models for a change, with winners and
losers. Risks: legal, cost, morale, precedent. Pay protection. What managers need so that they
can explain a pay decision.

**Outcome 4.** Measures of effect: turnover in the affected group, offers accepted, pay gap
figures, survey answers on fairness, cost against budget. Exceptions and special cases, and how
they become precedent. Own bias in advice on individual pay.

## Workplace scenario

Use this scenario for discussion, or for assessment if the learner cannot use a case from their
own work.

Aisling is the people manager of a software company in Leeds with 130 employees. The company has
no pay structure. Managers agree each salary with the candidate. New developers now join on more
than colleagues with four years of service. Two senior engineers resigned this quarter, and one
stayed after a counter-offer of 18 per cent. That news spread in a day. A first look at the
data shows that women in engineering earn less on average than men in the same job titles. The
founders fear that pay grades will make the company slow and corporate.

Tasks:

1. Analyse the causes of the pay problems and the risks if nothing changes.
2. Evaluate two approaches to a pay structure for this company, and recommend one.
3. Plan how to explain the change to the founders, the managers and the employees who gain
   nothing from it.

## Assessment

Suitable methods: work-based assignment, portfolio of work evidence, project report,
professional discussion, presentation, reflective account.

Evidence requirements:

- The evidence comes from the learner's own work on reward or recognition in a real
  organisation.
- It includes a market pay assessment for at least one role, an assessment of pay fairness
  between groups, and a costed plan for one change with its communication.
- Pay data is made anonymous. The learner combines small groups so that the pay of one person
  cannot be worked out.
- A learner with no authority over pay can meet outcome 3 with a proposal to the person who
  decides, and the response to it.
